Most marketing teams test everything. Ads, landing pages, subject lines, pricing pages. Then they reach AI search and stop testing. They ship pages, chase mentions and watch a score move without knowing why. This guide explains how we run AI search as a test programme. It covers what an experiment is, how we handle noisy answers, and how results tie back to pipeline.

What are AI search experiments?

An AI search experiment is a test of one change to how your brand shows up, measured on the answers AI engines give your buyers. We write a hypothesis, record the change and its date, rerun the same buyer prompts on Max Visibility, and compare before and after. Then we keep what moved the story AI tells and drop what didn’t.

Almost any piece of GEO work can be framed this way. These are the changes we test most often:

  • A positioning rewrite on your homepage and product pages.
  • A new, honest comparison or alternatives page.
  • Schema and structured data on key pages.
  • A review-site push, so recent buyers describe you in their own words.
  • A presence on YouTube or in communities like Reddit.
  • A PR mention or a place on a third-party list.

Each one costs time and money. Each one should earn its place by changing what ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity or Google’s AI answers say about you.

The method, not a button

One thing we say plainly: Max Visibility has no experiments screen. It tracks your buyer prompts, the full answer from each engine, your ranking against competitors and the sources each engine cites. The experiment is the method our strategists run on top of that data. We log the change, pick the prompts it should affect, and make the call.

That split matters. A tool can show you a line going up. It can’t tell you which of last month’s ten changes caused it. Our change log can.

Why treat every change as an experiment?

Because most AI search work is shipped on faith. Teams publish pages, earn mentions and fix schema, then watch a score drift and guess why. Treating each change as an experiment shows which work moved AI’s answer, on which engine, through which source. You stop paying for work that changes nothing, and you double down on work that does.

AI search is a positioning problem before it’s a technical one. An engine recommends the brands it can describe in one sentence: who they’re for, what they’re best at, why they’re different. There’s no page two in AI search. An answer names a handful of brands, and the rest don’t exist for that buyer.

So the question is never “did we publish enough?” It’s “did AI start telling our story?” Volume can’t answer that. Only a before and after can.

A tracker reports. An experiment answers.

A tracker tells you that you appeared in a share of answers this month. An experiment answers the questions a CMO actually asks. Did the new positioning land? On which engines? Because of which sources? Was it worth the budget?

Why do AI answers change from run to run?

AI engines write each answer fresh. The same prompt can name different brands, in a different order, citing different sources, minutes apart. Engines also update their models and how they retrieve pages. So one answer is an anecdote, not a measurement. We run each prompt several times on every engine, build a baseline of the normal spread, and only call a change that clears it.

This is the part most AI search reports skip. A screenshot of ChatGPT naming you proves that it happened once. It doesn’t prove it happens for most buyers, most of the time.

Where the variation comes from

  • Generation. Answers are written word by word, with some randomness built in.
  • Retrieval. Engines that search the web can pull a different set of pages on each run.
  • Location. The same prompt can answer differently by country, so we track by country where it matters.
  • Updates. Engines change models and ranking quietly. A shift can come from them, not from you.

Baselines and the noise band

Before we change anything, we freeze the prompt set and let it run on a daily schedule. That gives us a baseline: how often you’re named, where, and with which sources, when nothing has changed.

The baseline also shows the noise band. That’s how far each number moves on its own, from run to run. A result inside the band is “within noise”, however good it looks. We only call a change when it moves clearly beyond the band and holds.

What we won’t do

  • Call a win from a single run or a single engine.
  • Change prompt wording in the middle of a test.
  • Credit a change for movement that started before it shipped.

How do we build prompts that can’t flatter you?

We write prompts the way a cold buyer would type them, not the way your website talks. Every scored prompt leaves the answer open, so other brands can win it. We keep your product names and slogans out, include prompts we expect you to lose, and freeze the wording before the first run. A biased prompt set only measures your own words back at you.

Most tools suggest prompts by reading your website. That sounds helpful. But if your site says “built-in CRM”, the tool asks which product has a built-in CRM, and you win a question no buyer asks. Here are the principles we use instead.

  • Open questions only. “Best tool for X” or “alternatives to Y” can be won by anyone. “A vs B” can only be won by A or B, so we use it to check citations, never to score share of voice.
  • Situations, not labels. Buyers describe their problem, not their segment. We write “our forecast keeps missing after a CRM move”, not “best tool for RevOps”.
  • None of your vocabulary. We list your product names, coined categories and slogans, then check that no scored prompt uses them.
  • Branded prompts kept apart. When your name is in the question, you always win it. Those prompts are tracked, but never mixed into non-branded share of voice.
  • Prompts you’ll lose. At least 15% of the set covers ground you don’t own yet. A set you always win is a rigged instrument.
  • A fixed field. We track the full competitor set, not a flattering few. If the field changes, the baseline restarts.
  • A second pair of eyes. Someone who didn’t write your positioning reviews the set for leading questions before it runs.

We tag every prompt by buyer stage and topic. That lets one experiment read only the prompts it should affect, such as the shortlist prompts after a comparison page goes live.

How does an experiment run, step by step?

Six steps. We set a baseline on your frozen buyer prompts and write a hypothesis. We ship the change and log its date, then rerun the same prompts. We compare answers, share of voice, position and cited sources against the baseline. Then we decide: keep it, extend it or drop it. Each verdict feeds the next test.

  1. Baseline. The frozen prompt set runs on every tracked engine until we know the normal spread.
  2. Hypothesis. One sentence: if we change this, AI will say that, on these prompts.
  3. Change. Our team ships it: a page, a schema fix, a review push, a PR placement. We log what changed and the date.
  4. Rerun. The same prompts keep running on the same schedule. We give engines time to pick the change up.
  5. Compare. We read answers, share of voice, position and cited sources against the baseline, per engine.
  6. Decide. Keep what landed, extend what landed on some engines, drop what stayed within noise.

What makes a good hypothesis

A good hypothesis is specific enough to be wrong. “More content will help” can’t fail, so it can’t teach us anything. “A page for teams leaving a legacy tool will get us named on alternatives prompts in Perplexity” can fail. That’s the point.

Each one has three parts: the change, the prompts it should move, and the reading we expect to shift. If a change targets shortlist prompts, we don’t judge it on problem-stage prompts.

Where we can, we ship one change at a time on a given set of prompts. When two changes must ship together, we say so in the log and judge them as one.

A worked example (fictional brand, sample data)

Acme is a made-up data platform. Engines kept calling it “a cheap enrichment tool”. The hypothesis: if the site, reviews and press all say “the data platform built for RevOps”, AI will stop calling it cheap enrichment. The setup was 20 buyer prompts on five engines, with repeated runs against a 28-day baseline.

What do we measure before and after?

Four readings, all from Max Visibility. The answers themselves. Share of voice against your competitor set. Your position when you’re named. And the domains and URLs each engine cites. We read them per engine and per prompt tag, because a change can land on Perplexity and do nothing on Gemini. Then we read pipeline signals next to them.

Which optimization levers do we test?

Three groups. On-site: answer-ready pages, schema, crawl access for AI bots and llms.txt. Off-site: citations on the sources each engine already trusts, such as reviews, lists, press, YouTube and communities. And positioning consistency: the same one-sentence story everywhere AI looks. Each lever gets its own hypothesis, so we know which one moved the answer.

On-site: make your pages easy to quote

  • Answer-ready pages. The answer first, in plain words, then the detail. Honest comparison and alternatives pages for the prompts buyers ask at the shortlist stage.
  • Schema. Structured data that states who you are, what you sell and the questions you answer.
  • Crawl access. AI crawlers must be allowed in, and key content must be in the first HTML. Vercel’s December 2024 study of AI crawler traffic found that none of the major AI crawlers rendered JavaScript.
  • llms.txt. A proposed convention that gives AI a plain map of your site. It’s cheap to add, so we add it, and we treat any effect as something to test, not assume.

About 60% of GEO overlaps with SEO, and the best SEO is the bare minimum for GEO. Our GEO and AEO services cover this work in depth.

Off-site: earn the sources engines trust

Each engine leans on different sources. The Sources view shows which domains and pages each engine cites for your prompts. That’s our target list: the review sites, lists, publications, YouTube channels and communities that already shape the answer.

When ChatGPT names you, it stakes its own credibility on you. That’s a trust transfer, and you can’t buy your way into it. We don’t run paid media. We earn the citation, then test whether it moved the answer.

Positioning consistency: one story everywhere

AI pieces your story together from every source it reads. If your site says one thing, your review profile another and an old press kit a third, AI averages them into something vague. So we check that your claims, proof points and objection answers match everywhere. Our positioning and messaging work fixes the story itself. The experiment tells us when AI starts repeating it.

How do experiments connect to pipeline?

We read AI movement next to three business signals: demo requests, branded search and what buyers say on sales calls. None is perfect alone, because many AI answers send no click at all. Together they show whether a change that moved AI’s story also moved buyers. A win that never reaches pipeline gets less budget next quarter.

  • Demo requests. We add a simple “where did you hear about us?” field and read the AI mentions in it.
  • Branded search. When AI recommends you, buyers often search your name next. We watch branded search in Search Console after each change.
  • Sales-call mentions. Buyers tell your reps when ChatGPT or Perplexity suggested you. We ask your team to log it, and we read those notes.

We’re honest about the limits. AI attribution is still rough, and no single report proves cause. That’s why we look for the same direction across signals, after a dated change, beyond the noise band.

Revenue is the metric that matters. Visibility is how we get there, not the goal.

How is this different from agencies and AI visibility tools?

Content agencies mostly measure output: pages shipped and keywords ranked. AI visibility tools measure a score and leave the work to you. We do the work and test it. Our strategists ship the change, log it, rerun the baseline and tell you whether the story moved. Agencies sell content volume. Tools sell dashboards. We sell the story AI tells about you, and proof that it changed.

How do we start an experiment programme?

Usually with an AI perception audit, so we know what each engine says about you today. Then we agree your buyer prompts, freeze them and build the baseline. The first hypotheses come from the gaps the audit finds. From there, testing runs monthly alongside the GEO work, and we review what landed every quarter.

It works best for B2B SaaS and AI companies at roughly $5M to $100M ARR. The right owner is a marketing leader who answers for pipeline, and who can give a brand six months to build.

It’s not a fit if you need results this week, want to buy placement, or want keywords with a new name. AI trust compounds slowly. Brands compound. Hacks don’t.

Frequently asked questions

What is an AI search experiment?

It’s a test of one change to how your brand shows up in AI answers, such as a positioning rewrite, a comparison page or a review push. We log the change and its date, rerun the same buyer prompts on Max Visibility, and compare answers, share of voice, position and cited sources.

Does Max Visibility have an experiments feature?

No. Max Visibility tracks prompts, answers, rankings and cited sources on five engines. The experiment is our method on top of that data: we write the hypothesis, log the change, rerun the baseline prompts and make the call.

Why do AI answers change every time you ask?

Engines write each answer fresh and can retrieve different sources on each run. One screenshot proves little. We run every prompt repeatedly on every engine, build a baseline, and only call a change when it moves beyond the normal spread.

Which AI engines do you test on?

Max Visibility tracks ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews and Google AI Mode. We also optimize for Claude, but Claude isn’t part of the tracked baseline today, so we don’t report experiment verdicts for it.

How long does an experiment take?

Long enough to beat the noise. We need a baseline first, then time for engines to pick up the change. On-site changes often show sooner than off-site citations. Movement on target prompts usually shows within 60 to 90 days.

How do you connect experiments to pipeline?

We read AI movement next to demo requests, branded search and what buyers say on sales calls. Many AI answers send no click, so we look for the same direction across several signals instead of trusting one attribution report.

Do we have to change our positioning to run experiments?

No. Many tests are smaller: a schema fix, a comparison page, a review push. But if AI describes you vaguely, we usually start with an AI perception audit and positioning work, because the other changes repeat that story.

Is Max Visibility included?

Yes. Today our team runs Max Visibility for every client, and it’s included in every engagement. Your team can see the same projects and export the data.
